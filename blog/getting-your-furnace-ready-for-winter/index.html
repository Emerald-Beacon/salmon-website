Getting Your Furnace Ready for Winter

Nothing is more unpleasant on the first chilly day of the season than turning on your furnace only to be greeted by a blast of cool air. This is not a way to start a winter season...

Salmon HVAC
Published November 3, 2025  •  Updated September 24, 2026  •  8 min read

The fastest way to get your furnace ready for a Northern Utah winter is to change the filter, clear the space around the unit, test it on a warm day, and book a professional tune-up in September or early October — before the first cold snap and the December repair rush. Nothing is more unpleasant on the first chilly morning of the season than turning on your furnace and getting a blast of cool air instead of heat. The good news: the vast majority of these breakdowns are easily avoidable with a little planning in the weeks leading up to winter.

Your furnace is one of those things you never think about when it is working. It hums along in the basement or a closet, perfectly content — until one cold morning it decides it has had enough. Along the Wasatch Front, that first hard freeze usually shows up in mid to late October, and the higher valleys like Morgan and Ogden Valley cool off even sooner. Once the cold arrives, every HVAC company in Davis, Weber, and Salt Lake counties is buried in last-minute no-heat calls, and you could be bundled under blankets for days waiting on a repair. A few simple steps now keep you off that list.

Why Furnace Maintenance Matters

Think of furnace care as your "winter car tune-up." You would not drive a full year without an oil change or a tire check, and your heating system deserves the same attention — especially in Utah, where furnaces run hard for months against dry, cold, high-altitude air. A quick furnace check-up before the season saves money, prevents mid-winter breakdowns, and keeps your family safe.

Lower Energy Bills and Improved Efficiency

When your furnace runs the way it should, you stay warmer and pay less. Dirty filters, worn components, and inefficient operation quietly waste fuel all winter — money vanishing straight up the flue. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, replacing a clogged filter and keeping a system properly maintained can meaningfully cut energy use, and a tune-up catches small problems before they become expensive failures. If your bills always seem to climb once the heat kicks on, our guide to cutting your winter heating bills pairs perfectly with a fall tune-up.

Safety First: Preventing Carbon Monoxide Risks

Safety is the part of maintenance most homeowners overlook until it is too late. When a heat exchanger cracks on a furnace that has not been serviced, it can leak carbon monoxide — an odorless, colorless gas responsible for poisonings every winter. Make sure you have a working CO detector on every level of your home and outside sleeping areas. During a professional tune-up, a technician inspects the heat exchanger, measures CO levels, and confirms the system is venting safely before you rely on it for the season.

DIY Furnace Prep You Can Do This Weekend

1. Check and Replace the Air Filter

This is the simplest, highest-impact thing you can do yourself. Pull the current filter and hold it up to a light — if you can't see through it, replace it. Check it monthly and swap most standard 1-inch filters every one to three months. Utah's dry, dusty winter air and inversion-season particulates can load a filter faster than you'd expect, and a clogged filter is one of the most common reasons a furnace overheats and shuts itself off. If indoor dust and allergies are a problem in your home, our winter indoor air quality guide covers filter upgrades worth considering.

2. Clear the Space Around the Unit

Your furnace needs room to breathe. As part of fall cleanup, remove any boxes, paint cans, cleaning supplies, or stored items that have crept in around it over the summer — both for airflow and for fire safety. While you're at it, walk the house and make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains. Restricted airflow makes the whole system work harder and heat unevenly.

3. Test It on a Warm Day

Don't wait for the first freeze to find out your furnace won't start. On a mild day, set the thermostat to heat and bump it up a few degrees. It's normal to get a short burst of cool air while the burners warm the heat exchanger, but you should feel warm air within a few minutes. Listen for loud banging, grinding, or high-pitched squealing — those are not normal. If the furnace is slow to fire, makes odd noises, short cycles, or never delivers warm air, it's time to call a pro. Our guide on why a furnace runs constantly explains what abnormal cycling usually means.

4. Check the Thermostat and Batteries

Replace the batteries in any battery-powered thermostat and CO detectors now. If you have a programmable or smart thermostat, set a winter schedule so you're not heating an empty house all day. Small step, real savings over a long heating season.

What to Leave to the Professionals

Some tasks belong to a trained technician. If your furnace is more than ten years old, have it professionally inspected at least once a year. A Salmon HVAC technician can reach components you can't, check for gas leaks, measure carbon monoxide, clean the burners and flame sensor, verify safe venting, and confirm everything is calibrated for the season. For a full walkthrough of what a proper service includes, see our 8-point fall furnace prep checklist and our overview of a complete winter furnace tune-up.

Warning Signs You Shouldn't Ignore

Call a professional if you notice any of these: certain rooms never heat as well as others (often a ductwork or blower issue), the furnace short cycles on and off, energy bills spike without explanation, you smell gas or a persistent burning odor, or a pilot light burns yellow or flickers instead of steady blue. A yellow flame can signal incomplete combustion and carbon monoxide risk — shut off the gas and call for emergency service right away. If your system won't heat at all, our first-hour no-heat troubleshooting guide walks you through what to check before help arrives.

Should You Repair or Replace This Year?

If your furnace is 15 to 20 years old and you're facing another repair, fall is the right time to weigh replacement instead of buying a fourth or fifth repair. It's also worth knowing what's coming: the U.S. Department of Energy has finalized a rule requiring all new non-weatherized residential gas furnaces to hit a minimum of 95% AFUE efficiency, effective December 18, 2028 (today's minimum, set back in 2007, is 80%). The rule applies to furnaces manufactured after that date — not the one already in your home — so there's no need to rush. But if you're replacing anyway, moving to a high-efficiency furnace or a cold-climate heat pump now lets you plan on your own timeline and may qualify for rebates and tax credits. Financing is available to spread the cost.

The Best Time to Schedule in Northern Utah

Early fall is the sweet spot — book in September or early October, before the first cold snap and before every other homeowner in Centerville, Bountiful, Layton, Ogden, and Salt Lake City is calling at once. Schedule ahead and you get a better appointment window and the peace of mind that your system is ready for whatever the season brings.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I get my furnace ready for winter in Northern Utah?

Aim to have your furnace serviced in September or early October, before the first cold snap. Along the Wasatch Front and in Davis, Weber, Salt Lake, and Morgan counties, the first hard freeze typically arrives in mid to late October, and mountain valleys like Morgan and Ogden Valley cool off even sooner. Scheduling early means you beat the December call rush and aren't left waiting for a repair on the coldest night of the year.

How often should I change my furnace filter during the heating season?

Check your filter every month and replace most standard 1-inch filters every one to three months. Homes with pets, allergies, or heavy cooking may need changes more often, and Utah's dry, dusty winter air and inversion-season particulates can load a filter faster than you expect. A clogged filter forces the furnace to work harder, raises your energy use, and is one of the most common causes of a system that overheats and shuts itself off.

What is the biggest safety risk with an unmaintained furnace?

The biggest risk is a carbon monoxide leak from a cracked heat exchanger. Carbon monoxide is odorless and colorless, so a working CO detector on every level of your home is essential. During a professional tune-up, a Salmon HVAC technician inspects the heat exchanger, checks combustion and CO levels, and confirms the system is venting safely before you rely on it all winter.

Why is my furnace blowing cold air when I first turn it on?

A brief blast of cool air on startup is normal while the burners heat the exchanger, but sustained cold air usually points to a dirty filter, a thermostat set to the wrong mode, a pilot or ignition problem, or a system that is short cycling. Most of these are avoidable with a fall tune-up. If the furnace never warms up or short cycles, shut it down and call a professional.

Do I need to replace my furnace before the new 95% efficiency rule?

No. The U.S. Department of Energy rule requiring new non-weatherized gas furnaces to meet 95% AFUE takes effect December 18, 2028, and it applies to furnaces manufactured after that date — not to the unit already in your home. You can keep running and maintaining your current furnace. If yours is over 15 years old and needing frequent repairs, it's worth asking about a high-efficiency replacement or heat pump now so you can plan on your own timeline.
