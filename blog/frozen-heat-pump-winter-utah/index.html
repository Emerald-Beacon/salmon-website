Frozen Heat Pump in Winter: Why Your Outdoor Unit Ices Up & What to Do

A little frost on your outdoor heat pump during a Northern Utah cold snap is completely normal. A solid block of ice is not. Here's how to tell the difference, what you can safely do yourself, and when it's time to call for service.

Salmon HVAC
Published November 3, 2026  •  10 min read
Quick Answer
  • Light frost is normal. A heat pump's outdoor coil runs below freezing in heating mode, so frost forms — and the unit melts it off automatically during its defrost cycle.
  • A unit encased in ice is not normal. Thick ice over the coil, fan, or top of the cabinet — or frost that never clears — means the defrost cycle, airflow, or refrigerant isn't right.
  • The usual culprits: snow and debris blocking airflow, a dirty filter, a failed defrost sensor or control board, a stuck reversing valve, a dead fan motor, low refrigerant, or water dripping onto the unit and refreezing.
  • What you can safely do: switch to emergency/auxiliary heat, clear snow and debris, replace a dirty filter, and let the ice thaw. Never chip ice off the coil.
  • Ice keeps coming back, fan won't spin, or no heat? Call (801) 397-0030 or request service.

You glance out the window on a cold January morning and your outdoor heat pump looks like it belongs in a freezer — white with frost, maybe a ring of ice around the base. Before you panic: some of that is supposed to happen. A heat pump pulls heat out of cold outdoor air, which means the coil outside runs colder than the air around it. In damp, freezing Northern Utah weather, moisture condenses on that coil and freezes. Every heat pump ever built deals with this, and every one has a built-in answer for it.

The trouble starts when the ice stops melting — when a thin, lacy frost turns into a solid shell over the coil and fan. That's when efficiency falls off a cliff, your backup heat starts doing all the work (and running up your electric bill), and the compressor starts taking abuse it wasn't designed for. This guide walks through exactly what's normal, what isn't, why a heat pump freezes up in winter, and the specific steps a homeowner can take before — and instead of — an emergency service call.

Why a heat pump frosts up at all: the defrost cycle

In winter, your heat pump runs in heating mode, which flips the refrigerant flow so the outdoor coil absorbs heat from the outside air and the indoor coil releases it into your house. To pull heat from, say, 25°F air, the outdoor coil has to be colder than that air — often in the teens or lower. Any humidity in the air hits that cold surface and freezes, exactly the way dew freezes on your car.

Here's the clever part. Several times an hour in the right conditions, the heat pump runs a defrost cycle: it briefly reverses itself back into cooling mode to send warm refrigerant through the outdoor coil and melt the frost off, then switches back to heating. While it defrosts, the outdoor fan usually stops, and your indoor backup heat kicks on so you don't feel a cold draft. The signs of a healthy defrost are easy to spot once you know them:

  • Steam or vapor rising off the outdoor unit for a few minutes (people sometimes think the unit is on fire — it's just melting frost).
  • The outdoor fan stopping while the compressor keeps humming.
  • A "whoosh" or click as the system switches modes.
  • A puddle of melt-water near the base afterward.

So a light, even coat of frost that comes and goes is your heat pump working as designed. The question is what to do when it doesn't come off.

Normal frost vs. a real problem

Use this as your quick gut-check when you're standing in the driveway looking at the unit:

Normal (leave it alone)Not normal (needs attention)
Thin, even white frost on the coilThick ice encasing the whole coil or cabinet
Frost that clears within a cycle or twoFrost that never melts, or grows all day
A little melt-water or steamA solid block of ice over the fan blades or on top of the unit
Happens in cold, damp weatherHappens even in dry cold, or paired with weak/no heat indoors

The single most useful signal: does the unit ever defrost? If you never see steam, never hear it switch modes, and the ice just keeps building, the defrost function isn't doing its job — and that's worth tracking down before the ice damages the fan or the coil.

Why your heat pump is freezing up: the common causes

When a Northern Utah heat pump ices over and won't clear, it's almost always one of these. We've ordered them roughly from "you can probably fix this yourself" to "call a technician."

1. Blocked airflow — snow, debris, or a dirty filter

This is the most common cause we see, and the good news is it's the most fixable. A heat pump needs air moving freely through the outdoor coil to defrost properly. Pile snow against it, let autumn leaves mat around the base, or bury it in a drift off the roof, and the coil runs far colder than intended and frosts faster than it can clear. A dirty indoor air filter chokes airflow too, which throws off the whole system. In Utah, the classic version of this is a big storm that half-buries a ground-mounted unit overnight.

2. Water dripping onto the unit and refreezing

Very Utah-specific: snow melting off a roofline, a gutter downspout, or a dripping eave lands on the heat pump and refreezes into a glaze, especially during our freeze-thaw days where it's sunny at noon and single digits by midnight. A clogged condensate drain or a unit sitting too low can do the same thing by letting melt-water pool at the base and freeze solid.

3. A failed defrost sensor or control board

The defrost cycle is triggered by a sensor (and a control board) that decides when the coil needs melting. If the defrost thermostat/sensor gives a bad reading, or the control board fails, the unit simply never enters defrost — frost keeps accumulating with nothing to stop it. This is one of the two most common "it won't defrost" diagnoses, and it's a technician repair.

4. A stuck reversing valve

The reversing valve is the part that flips the system between heating, cooling, and defrost. If it sticks, the heat pump can't swing into defrost mode to melt the coil (and you may also notice the system blowing cool air when it should be heating). A technician can often confirm it by listening for the valve's click when the system should be switching modes.

5. A dead or weak outdoor fan motor

If the outdoor fan motor fails or weakens, it can't move enough air across the coil, and ice builds up fast. A fan that won't spin at all is a clear sign something's wrong — the same symptom shows up on the cooling side in summer, which we cover in our guide to an outside fan that won't spin.

6. Low refrigerant

Less common, but a system low on refrigerant (usually from a leak) runs its coil colder than it should and ices up. Refrigerant work is licensed, technician-only territory — if this is the cause, the leak needs finding and fixing, not just a "top-off."

What to do right now: a homeowner's step-by-step

If you're looking at an iced-up unit today, here's the safe order of operations. None of this requires tools or taking anything apart.

  1. Switch to emergency (auxiliary) heat. On your thermostat, set it to "emergency heat" or "aux heat." This keeps your home warming through the backup heat source (electric strips or, in a dual-fuel system, your gas furnace) and takes the load off the frozen heat pump while it thaws.
  2. Let it thaw — don't attack it. Give the ice time to melt. If you want to speed it up, run cool or lukewarm water from a hose or bucket over the unit. Never use hot or boiling water (it can crack parts and refreeze fast), and never chip, scrape, or hammer the ice — the coil's thin aluminum fins bend at a touch, and a damaged coil is a costly fix.
  3. Clear the area. Once it's thawed, shovel snow away from all sides and the top, and clear out leaves, grass, and debris. Aim for roughly 18–24 inches of clearance around the unit and a clear space above it.
  4. Check your furnace filter. A clogged filter starves the system of airflow. If it's dirty, replace it — it's the cheapest fix in HVAC.
  5. Look for a drip source. Is water running off the roof, a gutter, or an eave onto the unit? That refreeze cycle will keep happening until the drip is redirected.
  6. Switch back and watch. With everything clear and thawed, return the thermostat to normal heat and watch for a defrost cycle (steam, fan pausing). If the unit ices over again quickly, switch back to emergency heat and call for service — you've done your part, and the rest is a diagnosis.

One more note: running on emergency heat for a day while you sort this out is fine, but it's expensive to run for long (especially electric strip heat). It's a bridge to a repair, not a season-long plan. If you're mid-cold-snap with no heat at all, our heating emergency action plan walks through staying safe and warm until help arrives.

When to call a professional

Clearing snow and swapping a filter is homeowner territory. Everything past that isn't. Call Salmon HVAC if:

  • The unit never defrosts — no steam, no mode switch — even after you've cleared it.
  • Ice returns within a few hours of thawing.
  • The outdoor fan won't spin, or you hear grinding or buzzing.
  • The whole cabinet is encased in ice and the backup heat is carrying the whole house.
  • You suspect low refrigerant, or the system is blowing cool air when it should be heating.
  • There's no heat at all and it's cold out — that's an emergency HVAC call, any hour.

A technician will confirm the defrost sensor and control board are working, test the reversing valve and fan motor, check the refrigerant charge for leaks, and make sure the unit is mounted and draining correctly for a Utah winter. Catching a defrost fault early is a lot cheaper than replacing a coil or compressor that got wrecked running under a block of ice.

Preventing it next winter

Most freeze-ups are preventable with a few habits:

  • Keep it elevated and clear. Heat pumps should sit on a stand or bracket that keeps them above the typical snow line — important in snowier spots like Morgan and Ogden Valley. Clear snow off the unit after every storm.
  • Mind the drip line. Make sure roof runoff, gutters, and downspouts aren't dumping onto the unit.
  • Change your filter on schedule all winter.
  • Get a pre-season tune-up. A fall check catches a weak defrost sensor, a tired fan motor, or a low charge before the first hard freeze. It's the same logic behind a fall furnace prep visit.

And if you're still deciding whether a heat pump is even the right call for our climate, it absolutely can be — modern cold-climate units heat efficiently well below zero. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a heat pump to freeze up in winter?

A light coating of frost on the outdoor coil is completely normal when a heat pump runs in cold, damp weather — the coil operates below freezing to pull heat from the outside air, so moisture condenses and freezes on it. Your heat pump handles this automatically with a defrost cycle that briefly melts the frost off. What is not normal is thick ice that encases the coil, the fan blades, or the top of the cabinet, or frost that never clears. That points to a failed defrost cycle or an airflow or refrigerant problem and needs attention.

What causes a heat pump to ice over and not defrost?

The most common causes are blocked airflow (snow, leaves, or debris packed around the unit, or a dirty filter), a failed defrost sensor or control board that never triggers the defrost cycle, a stuck reversing valve, a dead outdoor fan motor, low refrigerant, or water from a roofline or clogged drain dripping onto the unit and refreezing. Blocked airflow and a faulty defrost control are the two culprits we see most often in Northern Utah.

Can I pour hot water on a frozen heat pump?

You can use lukewarm or cool water from a hose or bucket to melt ice off the unit — never boiling or hot water, which can crack components or warp coil fins as it refreezes. Even better, switch the system to emergency (auxiliary) heat and let the ice thaw on its own. Never chip, scrape, or hammer ice off the coil or fan; the aluminum fins bend easily and a damaged coil is an expensive repair.

Should I turn off my heat pump if it is covered in ice?

Yes. If the outdoor unit is encased in ice, switch your thermostat to emergency or auxiliary heat so your home keeps warming through the backup heat source while the heat pump thaws. Running a heavily iced unit can overwork the compressor and the fan motor. Once it is thawed and you have cleared any snow or debris, you can switch back — but if it ices over again quickly, leave it on backup heat and call for service.

How much does it cost to fix a heat pump that keeps freezing?

It depends on the cause. A cleaning and airflow fix may just be the cost of a service visit. A defrost control board or sensor typically runs a few hundred dollars installed (often roughly $200 to $500), an outdoor fan motor is similar, and a stuck reversing valve is a bigger refrigerant-circuit repair that can run from several hundred dollars to well over $1,000 once labor and refrigerant recovery are included.
